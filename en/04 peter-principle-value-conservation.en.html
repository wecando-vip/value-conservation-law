THE ANSWER FIRST · IN ONE SENTENCE

The Peter Principle is, at its core, "promotion that looks only at the E of the past, never at the S and T of the future": a person is promoted for competence, again and again, until landing in a position he or she cannot handle — and the organization fills with people "stuck in the wrong seat."

There is only one cure — turn promotion from "rewarding the past" into "investing in the future": calibrate E by competence, preserve S with a dual-track system, and lengthen T with training. Put people in the right seats, and value multiplies.

THE MULTIPLICATION LAW · PERSON–ROLE FIT
Y = E × S × T

E Role output · S Role fit (competence · expertise) · T Growth time (training · seasoning)

When fit, growth, and output advance together, value multiplies; competence is multiplication, incompetence is zero.

THE DIVISION LAW · WHERE PETER COMES FROM
E = Y / (S × T)

Promotion weighs only past performance (E), ignoring fit for the new role (S) and growth potential (T) — sending a capable doer into a management seat he cannot fill.

01

What Is the Peter Principle

"In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence."
在层级组织中，每个员工都会升到不能胜任的位置

Origin: In 1969, the Canadian educator Laurence J. Peter set it out in The Peter Principle. The core logic is simple: do well → get promoted → do well again → get promoted again → until you can no longer do well.

It is not about "universal mediocrity" — it is a systemic flaw in the promotion mechanism: rewarding past performance without testing future competence. A skilled technologist is promoted into a poor manager, a top salesperson into an ineffective director — the greater the E in the old role, the harder the fall in the new one.

How It Forms: The Mismatch in Promotion

MISFIT
FORMATION

Three "Onlys" Send the Capable into Roles They Cannot Fill

Only E (performance) counts → S (fit) is ignored → T (growth) is rushed. The Peter Principle begins to run.

DimensionBefore PromotionAfter Promotion

SBEFOREPerson and role fit tightlyAFTERWhat he excels at is no longer needed; what's needed he can't do — fit hits zero
TBEFOREAccumulation and growth in placeAFTERNo training, no buffer, straight into the seat — growth breaks off
FORMATION · PROMOTION MISMATCH

The Peter Principle is not "the person is incapable"; it is the wrong signal for promotion. Using old-role performance (E) as the main criterion is to ignore the new role's fit (S) and growth cycle (T) — dismantling the multiplication formula into division: the person is the same, yet the value is divided smaller by the new role. Every incompetent seat in an organization is the trace of one "E-only" promotion.

05

How to Break It: Three Paths of the Multiplication Formula

MULTIPLY
THE CURE

Weight Competence, Keep Experts, Grow People — Bring Promotion Back to Value

You cannot change human nature, but you can change the mechanism: E by competence, S via dual tracks, T through training.

REPAIR E · PROMOTE BY COMPETENCE
  • Look to the future: evaluate competence for the new role, not performance in the old one
  • Trial period: serve 3-6 months in the role first; actual performance decides the permanent seat
  • Up and down freely: those who don't fit may return to a professional track; "demotion" is not a disgrace
REPAIR S · DUAL TRACKS KEEP EXPERTS
  • Expert ladder: senior/principal expert grades — promotion and pay without becoming a manager
  • Management/professional tracks: those who love managing manage; those who love doing, do
  • Best use of talent: keep star players in high-value seats, reward with projects and incentives rather than transfer
REPAIR T · LET GROWTH CATCH UP WITH THE ROLE
  • Promotion is training: pair every promotion with management development, so T can reach the new role's demands
  • Coaches and mentors: assign new managers a senior mentor to shorten the competence curve
  • Long-term lens: treat promotion as investment, not reward — ask first, "Can he be competent in two years?"
IN ONE SENTENCE

The Peter Principle is not a curse on ability; it is a mirror of the mechanism — it reflects whether we promote by "past performance" or by "future competence." Put people right, and everyone becomes a multiplier.

07

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does the Peter Principle mean competent people shouldn't be promoted?

A: No. It means promotion should rest on competence, not on past performance alone. The capable deserve promotion — but into roles they can fill, not as a trade-in of old results for a new seat.

Q: Won't keeping top performers unpromoted make them leave?

A: It will — unless alternative incentives exist. That is the point of dual tracks: the expert ladder offers status and reward, so that "not managing" carries as much value as "managing."

Q: How can ordinary people use this formula?

A: It applies to career choices too: before taking a promotion, ask three questions — Am I competent for the new role (E)? Does it fit my strengths (S)? Do I have time to grow into it (T)? Only when all three are answered, say yes.
