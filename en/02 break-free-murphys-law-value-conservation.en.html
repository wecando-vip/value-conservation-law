THE ANSWER FIRST · IN ONE SENTENCE

Murphy's Law is the division formula running automatically through our lives: in pursuit of efficiency (E), we quietly compress fault tolerance (S) and slack (T), until low-probability mistakes become certainties through the accumulation of time.

There is only one cure — a return to the multiplication formula: keep fault tolerance, keep slack, and let errors be "non-fatal, repairable, learnable." Murphy's Law cannot be eliminated, but it can be designed around.

THE MULTIPLICATION LAW · A STABLE SYSTEM
Y = E × S × T

E Efficiency & gain · S Fault tolerance (backup · review) · T Slack (buffer · long-term)

Only when all three exceed 1 is a system stable; compress any one, and mistakes slide from "possible" to "certain".

THE DIVISION LAW · WHERE MURPHY COMES FROM
E = Y / (S × T)

When a person answers only to E, the instinct is to cut backups, skip reviews, and squeeze out every last minute of slack.

Shrink the denominator, and fault tolerance hits zero — and so, "whatever can go wrong, will."

01

What Is Murphy's Law

"Anything that can go wrong will go wrong."
凡是可能出错的事，就一定会出错

Origin: In 1949, U.S. Air Force engineer Edward Murphy, during a rocket-sled experiment, found that the test sensors had been wired backwards — and of all the possible ways to wire them, the worst one had occurred.

It is not about "bad luck" — it is a fact of probability: if something can possibly go wrong, then over enough repetitions, it will almost certainly go wrong. The universe is not singling you out; time simply accumulates coincidence into certainty.

Three Compressions Turn "Possible" into "Certain"

Compress S (fault tolerance) → compress T (slack) → inflate E (efficiency). The instant fault tolerance hits zero, Murphy's Law takes hold.

FORMATION · THE DIVISION LAW

Murphy's Law is not "probability suddenly rising"; it is the denominator being shrunk. S and T are a system's shock absorbers: backups let errors roll back, slack lets errors be repaired. Once the absorbers are removed, every small slip transmits straight into outcomes — and low-probability events inevitably surface over time. It is the epitome of "accountable only to E": the more we fear error, the bigger the error we make.

03

Murphy in Daily Life: We've All Been There

THE QUEUE

You switch lines, and the one you left instantly speeds up — it only "deserves" to after you leave.

THE UMBRELLA

It never rains when you carry one; the day you skip it, it pours — one miss makes it feel like "always."

RUNNING LATE

The more you rush, the more traffic and red lights — when slack T hits zero, any delay becomes a "disaster."

THE DEPLOY

The bigger the release and the tighter the deadline, the more bugs in production — the inevitable result of checking (S) compressed to the limit.

What the Books Say

The Black Swan
Nassim Taleb

Extreme events are more common than we imagine. Murphy's Law is the everyday version of the black swan: given enough time, low-probability events will occur — so prepare redundancy rather than pray it never comes.

Antifragile
Nassim Taleb

The goal is not "never make a mistake" but "not be hurt by mistakes." Turn errors into learning material and volatility into evolutionary pressure — this is the very logic of the multiplication formula.

The Checklist Manifesto
Atul Gawande

Even doctors forget steps. After Gawande introduced an intubation checklist in the ICU, infection rates fell about 66% — one checklist is a shock absorber restored to fault tolerance (S).

How to Break It: Three Paths of the Multiplication Formula

MULTIPLY
THE CURE

Fault Tolerance, Slack, and Learning — Make Errors Non-Fatal

Murphy's Law cannot be eliminated, but it can be designed around: E must be real, S must be thick, T must be long.

REPAIR S · PUT FAULT TOLERANCE BACK
  • Checklists: surgery checklists, pre-flight checks, deploy checklists — turn "relying on memory" into "relying on process"
  • Backups: the 3-2-1 rule for important files, two-person review for critical roles, a Plan B for every presentation
  • Redundancy: keep a mechanism that "catches you even when you fall," so errors can roll back
REPAIR T · LEAVE ROOM FOR SLACK
  • Schedule buffers: add 15-20% slack to project timelines, treating "surprise" as a fixed cost rather than an exception
  • Head start: arrive at the airport 2 hours early, show up early for big meetings — hedge uncertainty with time
  • Long-term view: swap "will this go wrong?" for "can I survive it wrong?" — from one-shot gambles to compounding gains
REPAIR E · RETHINK WHAT EFFICIENCY MEANS
  • Slow is smooth, smooth is fast: think before acting, get it right the first time — rework costs far more than the "saved" checking time
  • A culture of tolerance: allow small mistakes, punish concealment — turn errors into "transparent learning points"
  • Antifragility: let the system profit from volatility (Antifragile) — each mistake makes you a bit steadier
IN ONE SENTENCE

Murphy's Law is not fate — it is a mirror of design. It shows how much fault tolerance and slack we have compressed away. Start leaving room in the system, and it starts to lose its grip.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is Murphy's Law superstition?

A: No. It is a law of probability and system design: if error is possible, enough repetitions make it inevitable. Its reminder is not "don't try" — it is "leave room."

Q: Why does worrying about error make error more likely?

A: Excessive worry compresses judgment and slack (T), making us act hastily and skip checks (S) — precisely the conditions that trigger Murphy's Law.

Q: How can ordinary people use this formula?

A: Use checklists for small things (S), leave buffers for big things (T), and review before moving on after mistakes — three habits that turn "always breaking" into "breaking without panic."
